Living in Sutton, MA

Five villages, old mill sites,and a granite chasm in the southeast.

Sutton is a Blackstone Valley town of 9,357 people (2020 Census) in Worcester County, made up of five villages: Sutton Center, Manchaug, Wilkinsonville, West Sutton and South Sutton. The town's 2025 Housing Production Plan reports that 86% of homes are single-family detached houses. Route 146 and Route 122A run through town, and commuter rail is reached at Grafton and Worcester stations on the Framingham/Worcester Line.

About Sutton

Sutton began as an eight-mile-square tract that the Massachusetts General Court granted to Edward Pratt and other proprietors in 1704, after a Nipmuc man, John Wampas, had deeded land in the area to Pratt. The Massachusetts Historical Commission dates the town's establishment to 1714, with settlement beginning around 1716 and the common and cemetery laid out in 1719. The town was much larger then: a parish set off in 1778 became Ward, later Auburn, and the northern section became Millbury in 1813. Through the 1700s Sutton was a town of dispersed farmsteads on drumlin hilltops, and the town had some of the county's most agriculturally productive soils. Population peaked at 2,718 in 1855.

Water power brought industry in the 1820s. David Wilkinson built a stone factory in 1823 at what became Wilkinsonville; the Blackstone Canal, opened in 1828, passed the village with a lock and basin until it closed in 1848, and the Providence and Worcester Railroad came through in 1847. On the Mumford River, investors from Providence bought the Manchaug site in 1826, and granite mills went up there in 1852, 1862 and 1868; the Manchaug mills closed in 1921. Sutton Center kept the civic role, with the 1829 Congregational Church and Rufus Putnam Hall, an 1824 school and Masonic lodge, while West Sutton grew along the Central Turnpike, opened around 1824. Sutton Center, West Sutton and Manchaug are each National Register historic districts. To the southeast, Purgatory Chasm cuts a deep rift through granite bedrock.

Sutton's 2025 Housing Production Plan, written by a town working group with help from the Central Massachusetts Regional Planning Commission and approved by the state in January 2026, counts 3,612 housing units in 2020 and reports that 86% of homes are single-family detached and 87% are owner occupied. About 15% were built before 1940. The town's Subsidized Housing Inventory stood at 55 units, or 1.55%, against the state's 10% goal, and the plan notes that recent building permits have all been for single-family homes. Public water reaches about 15% of the town's land through the Wilkinsonville, Manchaug and Whitinsville water districts. Under the MBTA Communities law, Sutton is classed as an adjacent small town that must zone for at least 181 multi-family units; the overlay brought to the Fall 2025 Town Meeting was recommended for parcels at 10 and 17 Galaxy Pass and 15 Pleasant Valley Road, and the state lists Sutton as compliant as of October 21, 2026.

Homes in Sutton

Sutton's 2025 Housing Production Plan counts 3,612 housing units in 2020 and cites American Community Survey estimates that 86% of homes are single-family detached and 87% are owner occupied. About 15% of homes were built before 1940, and the plan puts the average age of a home at about 56 years. Subsidized housing makes up 55 units, or 1.55% of the year-round stock, and recent building permits have all been for single-family homes.

Neighborhoods and villagesSutton

Where you mightlive in Sutton.

Sutton Center

The civic village where Boston Road, Singletary Avenue and Uxbridge Road meet, around the common laid out in 1719; its 435-acre National Register district, listed in 2001, has more than 120 contributing resources, including the 1829 Congregational Church and an 1839 brick commercial block.

Manchaug

A textile village on the Mumford River, centered where Main Street meets Manchaug, Putnam Hill and Whitins Roads, with large stone mills and gabled, multi-family wood-frame worker houses; it was listed on the National Register in 2018.

Wilkinsonville

The village that grew around David Wilkinson's 1823 stone factory, where the Blackstone Canal had a lock and basin and the Providence and Worcester Railroad arrived in 1847; it also has a 1917 school building.

West Sutton

A turnpike village along the Central Turnpike from Manchaug Road to the Oxford line, in a 460-acre National Register district of 18th- and 19th-century houses, farmsteads, a church, a cemetery and an 1831 sawmill.

South Sutton

A small cluster of houses along Whitin Road, south of the Second Baptist meetinghouse.

Getting around

Commuting from Sutton

Sutton has no commuter rail station. Grafton station, at 1 Pine Street in North Grafton, and Worcester station are both on the MBTA Framingham/Worcester Line. Route 146, the Worcester-Providence highway, runs north and south through town, and Route 122A follows Providence Road, improved as a regional highway in the 1920s.

Things to doSutton

Places to knowin Sutton.

Purgatory Chasm State Reservation

A DCR reservation at 198 Purgatory Road with about two miles of trails to rock formations named the Corn Crib, the Coffin and Lovers' Leap; the Chasm Trail itself closes in winter.

Waters Farm

A farm at 53 Waters Road with a Georgian house built by Stephen Waters in 1757 and 120 acres given to the town in 1974; Waters Farm Preservation, Inc. maintains it as an open-air museum, and it was listed on the National Register in 1985.

Marion's Camp

The town beach at the end of Tuttle Road, with a pavilion, trails and a kayak launch; staff are on site from mid-June through Labor Day and a beach pass is required then.

Manchaug Mill #2 and #3 site

Town-owned mill ruins on the Mumford River, with granite blocks, a chimney foundation, a 1920 bridge and a stone arch bridge over the tailraces; a December 2024 CMRPC concept plan proposes an interpretive park with trails and a river overlook.

Sutton Town Common

The common on Uxbridge Road in the Sutton Center Historic District; the town's FY2026 capital plan sets aside $30,000 to restore its bandstand.

Edward Putnam House

A house at 211 Putnam Hill Road built in 1737 and pictured on the town seal.

Blackstone Canal Historic District

The canal corridor from Blackstone to Worcester, listed on the National Register in 1995, which passes through Wilkinsonville, where the canal had a lock and basin.

What is the median home price in Sutton, MA?

The median single-family sale price in Sutton was $615,000 in calendar year 2025, across 70 sales, down from $639,900 across 80 sales in 2024, according to The Warren Group's MA December 2025 Sales, Prices by Town report. The condo median was $562,933 across 12 sales.

Does Sutton have commuter rail?

Not within town lines. Grafton station in North Grafton and Worcester station are both on the MBTA Framingham/Worcester Line.

Which school district serves Sutton?

Sutton Public Schools. Review its profile with DESE.

What is the property tax rate in Sutton?

Sutton's FY2026 rate is $11.55 per $1,000 of assessed value, one rate for all classes of property. Homes in the Wilkinsonville or Manchaug water districts also pay a district rate of $0.31 or $1.64 per $1,000.

What are the villages of Sutton?

The town's 2025 Housing Production Plan names five villages: Sutton Center, Manchaug, Wilkinsonville, West Sutton and South Sutton. Sutton Center, West Sutton and Manchaug are National Register historic districts.

Is Sutton covered by the MBTA Communities zoning law?

Yes. The state classes Sutton as an adjacent small town that must zone for at least 181 multi-family units, and its list of compliant municipalities, as of October 21, 2026, includes Sutton.
