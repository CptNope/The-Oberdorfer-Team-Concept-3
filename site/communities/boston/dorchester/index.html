Living in Dorchester, Boston, MA

Squares strung along the Red Line,three-deckers between them, the Neponset at the edge.

Dorchester is Boston's largest neighborhood, more than 6 square miles between South Boston and the Neponset River, founded as a separate town in 1630 and annexed to Boston in stages that ended in 1870. Its housing mixes single-family, two-family and three-decker homes and condominiums, and the Boston Planning & Development Agency counted 122,191 people there in 2020. Five Red Line stations, the Mattapan Line and Fairmount Line commuter rail stations including Uphams Corner, Four Corners/Geneva and Talbot Avenue serve it.

About Dorchester

Where it is

Dorchester is bordered by South Boston, the South End, Roxbury, Jamaica Plain and Hyde Park; the Neponset River separates it from Quincy and Milton, and Dorchester Bay lies to the east. It is often divided into North Dorchester, the part north of Quincy, East and Freeport Streets, and South Dorchester.

Settlers who came on the Mary and John landed at Columbia Point in June 1630 and founded Dorchester months before Boston. The town held what is described as the first town meeting in what became the United States on October 8, 1633, buried its dead in the North Burying Ground at today's Uphams Corner from 1634, and opened the Mather School in 1639. Israel Stoughton built a grist mill on the Neponset River in 1634, and from 1765 chocolate was refined at Lower Mills, where the Walter Baker company made it until 1965. Boston took Dorchester in pieces, starting with Dorchester Neck (now South Boston) in 1804, and annexed the rest of the town in 1870.

Dorchester grew as a streetcar suburb. The Old Colony Railroad crossed it from 1845, Dorchester Avenue carried a horse-drawn streetcar, and by 1890 the streetcar and the three-decker had come together here, with three-deckers filling the land between the old village centers. Those centers are still the neighborhood's commercial squares: Uphams Corner and Bowdoin/Geneva in the north; Fields Corner, Codman Square, Peabody Square at Ashmont, Adams Village and Lower Mills to the south. The Red Line's Dorchester stations opened in 1927 and 1928, from Columbia (now JFK/UMass) through Savin Hill, Fields Corner and Shawmut to Ashmont.

Columbia Point, where the first settlers landed, now holds UMass Boston, which moved there in 1974, the John F. Kennedy Presidential Library, dedicated in 1979, the Edward M. Kennedy Institute and the Massachusetts Archives. The Fairmount Line gained new Dorchester stations at Talbot Avenue in November 2012 and Four Corners/Geneva in July 2013, and Pope John Paul II Park opened in 2001 on a former drive-in and dump along the Neponset. In May 2024 the Boston Planning Department began a Squares + Streets plan for Fields Corner, part of a citywide program covering housing, public space, small businesses and transportation in neighborhood centers; the department's project page showed it still in progress, with no adoption date.

Homes in Dorchester

Dorchester's housing includes single-family, two-family and three-family houses and condominiums. Three-decker apartment buildings went up in large numbers alongside the streetcar lines from around 1890, and Victorian houses fill hills such as Savin Hill, where lots were sold after the Civil War. The Boston Planning & Development Agency's tabulation of the 2016-2020 American Community Survey counts 44,979 occupied housing units, 37.3% of them owner-occupied, compared with 35.3% citywide.

Corners ofDorchester.

Savin Hill

A hill above Dorchester Bay, first called Rock Hill and renamed in 1819, built up with Victorian houses after the Civil War; it has a Red Line station from 1927 and Savin Hill and Malibu beaches nearby.

Fields Corner

A commercial district centered on the X-shaped crossing of Dorchester Avenue and Adams Street, named for Zechariah Field, with the 1874 Fields Corner Municipal Building and a Red Line station; side streets hold three-deckers and Victorian houses.

Ashmont

The area around Ashmont station at Dorchester Avenue and Ashmont Street, the Red Line's southern terminus on this branch and the transfer point to the Mattapan Line, with Peabody Square's shops beside it.

Lower Mills

The old mill village on the Neponset River at the Milton line, where the Walter Baker chocolate buildings, a major part of them built from 1868, now hold mixed industrial, retail and residential uses; the district was listed on the National Register in 1980.

Uphams Corner

The square where Columbia Road meets Dudley and Stoughton Streets, named for grocer Amos Upham, with the 1918 Strand Theatre, the 1634 North Burying Ground and a Fairmount Line station.

Codman Square

The crossing of Washington Street and Talbot Avenue, a National Register district since 1983 that includes the 1806 Second Church in Dorchester and the 1904 Codman Square branch library; Talbot Avenue station on the Fairmount Line is nearby.

Getting around

Commuting from Dorchester

The Red Line's Ashmont branch has five stations in Dorchester, and at Ashmont it meets the Mattapan Line trolley, which stops at Cedar Grove in southern Dorchester. The Fairmount Line runs from South Station through Newmarket, Uphams Corner, Four Corners/Geneva and Talbot Avenue toward Readville. I-93, Route 203 and Route 28 (Blue Hill Avenue) cross the neighborhood, along with Gallivan Boulevard and Morton Street.

  • Red Line (Ashmont branch): JFK/UMass, Savin Hill, Fields Corner, Shawmut, Ashmont
  • Mattapan Line: Ashmont (transfer), Cedar Grove
  • Fairmount Line: Uphams Corner, Four Corners/Geneva, Talbot Avenue

Check current schedules with the MBTA; compare the trip from each address before you buy.

Schools

Public schools

Dorchester is served by Boston Public Schools. School assignment depends on the address and the district's own rules; confirm it with the district, and review its profile with the Massachusetts Department of Elementary and Secondary Education.

Places to knowin Dorchester.

John F. Kennedy Presidential Library and Museum

I. M. Pei's library on a 9.5-acre site at the tip of Columbia Point, beside UMass Boston, dedicated on October 20, 1979, with a glass and steel pavilion 115 feet high.

James Blake House

Built in 1661, a date confirmed by tree-ring dating in 2007, and described as the oldest surviving house in Boston; it stands at 735 Columbia Road in Edward Everett Square and is owned by the Dorchester Historical Society.

Pope John Paul II Park

A 66-acre DCR park on the Neponset River, opened in 2001 on the site of the Neponset Drive-In and the Hallet Street dump, with trails including the Lower Neponset River Trail.

Franklin Park

Olmsted's 485-acre park, Boston's largest open space, which connects Dorchester with Roxbury, Jamaica Plain, Mattapan and Roslindale; it holds the Franklin Park Zoo and the William Devine Golf Course.

Strand Theatre

A theater at Uphams Corner, opened in 1918 as one of the first built specifically for motion pictures.

Dorchester North Burying Ground

A burying ground at Uphams Corner dating to 1634, one of the oldest in the country.

Dorchester-Milton Lower Mills Industrial District

The Walter Baker chocolate complex on both banks of the Neponset, where chocolate was made from 1765 until 1965, now in mixed industrial, retail and residential use.

The Boston city guide

What is the median home price in Dorchester?

According to The Warren Group's MA December 2025 Sales, Prices by Town table, which lists Dorchester on its own row, the median single-family sale price for calendar year 2025 was $800,000, across 103 sales. In 2024 it was $730,000, across 110 sales. Much of Dorchester's housing is two- and three-family buildings and condominiums, which this single-family figure does not cover, and condo figures could not be read from the table. The same report's condominium median for Dorchester was $575,000 in 2025, across 360 sales, against $580,000 across 377 sales in 2024.

Does Dorchester have commuter rail?

Yes. The Fairmount Line stops at Uphams Corner, Four Corners/Geneva and Talbot Avenue in Dorchester on its way between South Station and Readville. The Red Line also has five Dorchester stations, from JFK/UMass to Ashmont.

Which school district serves Dorchester?

Boston Public Schools. School assignment is set by the district; review its profile with DESE.

What are Dorchester's main squares?

Dorchester's commercial districts grew from its old village centers: Uphams Corner and Bowdoin/Geneva in the north, and Fields Corner, Codman Square, Peabody Square at Ashmont, Adams Village and Lower Mills in the south. Residential sections include Savin Hill, Jones Hill, Meeting House Hill, Ashmont Hill, Neponset, Port Norfolk, Popes Hill and Cedar Grove.
